The safety question

Do you need a meat thermometer if your air fryer has presets?

Presets guess. A thermometer measures. The USDA minimum internal temperatures, what each type of thermometer is for, and when a preset is good enough.

Yes, if you cook meat, poultry, fish or leftovers. A preset is a guess at the time and temperature needed; the USDA sets minimum internal temperatures specifically to be measured with a food thermometer, and no preset can know the thickness of the piece in your basket.

5 min read · specification-led · sources S10 S06 S07

What the USDA actually says

The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service publishes minimum internal temperatures to be reached before food is removed from the heat source, measured with a food thermometer. The figures that matter most for air frying: all poultry, including ground poultry, 165 °F (73.9 °C); ground meats 160 °F (71.1 °C); beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts 145 °F (62.8 °C) with a three-minute rest; fish and shellfish 145 °F (62.8 °C); leftovers and casseroles 165 °F (73.9 °C).

The FSIS page is explicit that these are minimums measured with a thermometer, and that cooking to higher temperatures is a matter of personal preference. A preset cannot perform that measurement.

Why a preset is still useful

A preset gives you a starting time and temperature that is usually in the right region, which saves you from guessing from zero. It is a starting point, not a completion signal.

Use it to plan when to start, and the thermometer to decide when to stop.

Which food genuinely needs the measurement

Whole chicken and thick pork are the cases where time-based cooking fails most visibly, because the outer layer overcooks while the centre is still under. Thin items like a burger patty are more forgiving but are exactly where a fast-read thermometer costs the least effort.

If you only ever cook frozen fries and vegetables, none of this applies to you. Air fryer presets are perfectly adequate for food that does not carry a pathogen risk.

The instant-read is the cheap version of this decision

A handheld instant-read thermometer answers the question in one second and costs a fraction of a wireless probe set. It is the right first purchase if you are unsure whether you will use a thermometer at all.

The wireless multi-probe replaces it only when you want to monitor without opening the machine — which is a different problem, covered in the thermometer buying guide.

The recommendation

Buy a handheld instant-read thermometer before you buy any other accessory. It is the lowest-cost way to make every cook reproducible and to know when food is safe rather than merely browned.

It changes if: If you already own an instant-read and cook long, slow cuts where you want to watch the temperature curve, buy a probe instead — see the thermometer buying guide.
