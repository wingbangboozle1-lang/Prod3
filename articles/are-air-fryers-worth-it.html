Are air fryers worth it? Six questions to ask before you decide

Kitchen Counter desk · Published 2026-11-20 · Figures are the makers’ published numbers, not test results · Six questions, about six minutes

“Worth it” has no general answer. It depends on the meals you cook, the counter you can give up, and what the machine would replace — and none of that is on a product listing. What is on the listing is capacity, footprint, temperature range and layout. This guide turns those published numbers into six questions you can answer about your own kitchen. Answer all six and the decision usually makes itself.

1. What would it replace on your counter?

An air fryer earns its footprint only if it takes over jobs from something else — or from nothing. The three air fryers we track list exterior depths of 12.75 in, 13.38 in and 16.9 in, and depths that different are not a rounding error. Measure the actual spot first, including the clearance each maker’s manual requires (the listings publish exterior dimensions, not operating clearance). The cleanup and counter-space guide shows the measurement method.

2. Do you cook two things at once, or one thing well?

Layout decides this, and layout is published. The Chefman 6 Qt Dual listing describes two independent 3-quart baskets with Sync Finish; the Chefman 8 Qt listing describes one deep basket; the Emeril 26QT listing describes a single oven cavity with French doors. Two zones ask less of your timing; one big basket asks less of your washing up. Neither is better in the abstract — better for a meal is the only comparison that means anything, and the capacity checklist is honest about what a quart label cannot tell you.

3. What temperature range does your cooking actually use?

Listed settings separate these machines more sharply than the word “air fryer” does. The two basket listings run 200–450°F and 200–400°F (the 8 Qt title also claims a 450°F Hi-Fry setting — both readings are published on Flagged for Review). The oven-style listing runs 75–500°F. If you want low-temperature functions — proofing, keeping warm, slow work — the low end matters more than the crisping end, and only one of the three publishes a number below 200°F.

4. Will the cleanup habit stick?

A machine that is annoying to clean becomes a cupboard ornament. Count the parts you would wash after a normal meal: two baskets and two trays, one basket and one tray, or an oven cavity plus its named accessories. The listings name their removable parts; whether those parts are dishwasher-safe is stated per part on each listing and in each manual — check the part, not the assumption. More detail is in the cleanup guide.

5. Does your oven already do this job?

Be honest here, because the honest answer saves money either way. A full-size oven already bakes, roasts and holds heat; what a countertop air fryer adds is a smaller cooking space with air moving close to the food. The oven-style unit is the closest substitute on paper — its cavity and 75–500°F range overlap the most with a conventional oven — while the basket units do crisping and reheating better than they do baking. If none of those jobs is a regular pain point in your kitchen, the honest answer to “are air fryers worth it” is not for you, right now.

6. Does the listing agree with itself?

This is the question almost nobody asks, and it is the cheapest one to answer. Read the title, the bullets and the details table as three separate documents. In the five listings we track, they disagree more than once: one title says 6 quarts while its details table says 6 liters (F-01); another claims 450°F in the title while its specification table says 200–400°F (both readings are kept visible on the Chefman 8 Qt page). A listing that contradicts itself is not necessarily a bad product — but it is a listing whose numbers deserve a question to the seller before you rely on them.

The short version. Measure the counter, pick the layout that matches your meals, check the temperature range against your cooking, count the parts you will wash, and cross-examine the listing. If the answers still point at buying, the “worth it” question has answered itself — and the side-by-side comparison is where to choose between the three.
Is an air fryer cheaper to run than a full-size oven?

This site publishes no energy-cost figures. All three air fryer listings state 1700W at 120V, but running cost depends on cook time and your local electricity rate, and neither is published here. Compare the listed wattage and your own usual cook times rather than a savings claim.

Can an air fryer replace a toaster oven?

They overlap but are not the same machine. The oven-style listing here runs 75–500°F and includes named baking accessories; the basket listings start at 200°F. Whether the overlap covers your own cooking is a question about your meals, not a spec. The footprint comparison goes deeper.

What size air fryer do I need?

A quart label describes the container, not the portion. Compare basket layout and interior dimensions with the meals you actually cook; no listing on this site establishes a serving size. The capacity checklist walks through it.
