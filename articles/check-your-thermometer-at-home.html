How do you know your meat thermometer is telling the truth?

Kitchen Counter desk · Published 2026-11-20 · Method and figures read from USDA FSIS the same day · Not a laboratory procedure

Every thermometer recommendation on this site rests on a number printed by a seller: ±0.5°F on one listing, a one-second read on the same one. Those are claims in a product page, and we say so everywhere we use them. Your own thermometer is different. You can check it in your kitchen with a glass, ice and tap water, and the answer you get is better evidence than any spec sheet.

What USDA expects of an ordinary thermometer

The Food Safety and Inspection Service’s Food Thermometers factsheet states the baseline plainly: “Most food thermometers will give an accurate reading within 2 to 4 degrees F.” It adds that a reading is only correct if the thermometer is in the right place, and that the same device is “the only reliable way” to confirm food has reached a safe minimum internal temperature.

Hold those two sentences together with a listing that advertises ±0.5°F. Either figure is a statement about the instrument, not about your dinner. What the instrument does in your kitchen is a question you can answer yourself.

The ice-water test

FSIS describes two methods. The one that suits most household thermometers is ice water:

  1. Fill a large glass with ice — the factsheet says finely crushed or cubed ice — then add clean water to the top of the ice and stir well.
  2. Put the stem in the mixture far enough to reach the sensing point. FSIS gives a minimum of two inches for the ice-water method, touching neither the sides nor the bottom of the glass.
  3. Wait at least 30 seconds without removing the stem, then read it.
  4. The reading should be 32°F. If it is not, adjust according to the manufacturer’s instructions — some dials carry a calibration nut, some digital units offer a reset button in the manual, and some offer nothing at all.

The boiling-water method exists for the high end of the range: water boiling at sea level is 212°F, and FSIS’s worked logic is arithmetic rather than judgement — if the display reads 214°F, it is running two degrees high, so subtract two from readings until you replace it. Boiling water is also how you check a probe rated for a grill.

When an offset is not a fix

Two honest limits. First, FSIS notes that if a thermometer cannot be calibrated you may need to replace it; a fixed offset is workable on a dial you read yourself and awkward on a device whose whole point is a number on a screen. Second, a thermometer that passes the ice test today says nothing about drift in a year; the test is cheap, so the sensible cadence is before the meals that matter rather than on a schedule someone abandons.

Do this before the holiday, not during it. Four minutes on Sunday, with a glass in the sink, and you know whether to trust the probe on Thursday. If the ice test fails and the unit will not adjust, that is information about the product, not about you.

Placement, because a good probe in the wrong place is a bad reading

FSIS: thickest part of the food, away from bone, fat and gristle. Thin foods get the stem inserted through the side until it reaches the centre. A roast or an irregular cut gets checked in several places, because size and distribution mean pieces finish at different times. Before any of that, read the maker’s instructions for insertion depth; if they are gone, look for the dimple on the stem, which marks one end of the sensing device.

Should a good thermometer read exactly 32°F in ice water?

That is the target of the test as FSIS describes it, and the same factsheet sets ordinary expectations at two to four degrees. Judge the instrument against both numbers, not against a marketing figure.

Does an ice-water test replace the maker’s calibration certificate?

No, and it does not need to. The certificate is a document about a measurement someone else made on one unit at one time; the test is about the unit in your hand today. We have examined neither certificate for the products on this site.

My digital thermometer has no dial to turn. Is it broken?

Not necessarily. Some digital units are adjusted in software or not at all, and FSIS’s guidance is to follow the manufacturer’s instructions or replace the device. Check the manual before deciding the reading is wrong.
