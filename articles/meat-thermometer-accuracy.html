What ±0.5°F means—and what a NIST claim does not tell you

Kitchen Counter · Editorial correction 2026-11-19 · Seller claims are not independent test results

The ThermoMaven 1 Sec listing advertises “±0.5°F accuracy,” uses the words “NIST & NSF Certified,” and says a calibration certificate is included. Those are claims in the seller's listing, not results from a Kitchen Counter test. We have not independently examined the included certificate or tested a unit.

What the ±0.5°F number could mean

Read ±0.5°F as the seller's stated accuracy limit, subject to the conditions and temperature range the seller specifies. The number alone does not tell you how it was tested, what uncertainty applies, whether it holds across the full range, or how a particular unit performs. We have not verified those details, so we do not treat the listing's figure as a measured result.

What NIST traceability does—and does not—establish

NIST defines metrological traceability as a property of a measurement result, not of an instrument, laboratory, or certificate by itself. NIST says it certifies traceability for results that NIST itself provides, directly or through an official program or collaboration; that certification does not automatically extend to measurements a buyer later makes with an instrument. See NIST's traceability FAQ and policy.

So a product-page phrase such as “NIST certified” is not, by itself, proof that NIST tested or endorsed this retail thermometer, nor proof that a buyer's future readings are traceable to NIST. The listing also mentions NSF; we have not verified that separate claim. To assess the included certificate, ask who performed the calibration, which instrument or result it covers, the date, range, uncertainty, and documented reference chain. We have not confirmed any of those details for this product.

Accuracy is not the same as food safety

A narrow stated error band does not replace correct probe placement or the recommended cooking temperature. USDA FSIS lists 145°F with a three-minute rest for whole cuts of beef, pork, veal, and lamb; 160°F for ground meats; and 165°F for poultry. See the USDA FSIS safe minimum internal temperature chart. FSIS also advises placing a thermometer in the thickest part and avoiding bone, fat, and gristle; follow the official placement guidance and the thermometer maker's instructions.

Read-time claims are a separate question

The product listing states a one-second read. That is a response-time claim, not evidence of the accuracy, repeatability, or calibration of a reading. It may be a useful feature to compare, but we have not timed or independently tested this device.

A careful checklist: distinguish the stated error band from a test result; ask for the temperature range, method, uncertainty, and certificate details; check probe placement and official food-safety temperatures. Unknowns stay unknown until supported.
Does “NIST certified” mean NIST approved this retail thermometer?

Not on the strength of that listing phrase alone. NIST's policy describes traceability of measurement results and says NIST certifies traceability only for results it provides. The seller's product claim is not an independent NIST product approval. Read NIST's explanation.

Does ±0.5°F mean a food is safe once the display reaches a target?

No. It is a seller-stated accuracy claim, not a food-safety recommendation or an independent test. Use the USDA FSIS temperature chart, correct placement, and the device's instructions.
